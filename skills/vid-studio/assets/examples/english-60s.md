# The last ten years do most of the work

## Hook
Your money earns more in its last ten years than in its first twenty. [3]
Visual: compare | ₹1 lakh at 12% a year | Years 1–20: ₹8.6 lakh | Years 21–30: ₹20.3 lakh
Source: Worked example, 12% a year

## How it works
That's because each year's return joins the pile, and next year's twelve percent is worked out on the bigger pile. [1]
Visual: list | Returns on returns | ₹1 lakh earns 12% | The 12% joins the pile | Next year earns on both

## Year one vs year thirty
In year one, twelve percent of a lakh is twelve thousand rupees. By year thirty it's twelve percent of about twenty-seven lakh, over three lakh in one year. [3]
Visual: compare | What 12% adds in one year | Year 1: ₹12,000 | Year 30: ₹3,21,000

## A rule you can use
A quick way to see the pace is the rule of seventy-two. Divide seventy-two by the rate, and at twelve percent your money doubles about every six years. [2]
Visual: stat | 6 years | to double at 12% | Rule of 72: 72 ÷ your rate
Source: Rule of 72 approximation

## The doublings
So one lakh turns into two, then four, eight, sixteen, and about thirty. Each doubling adds more than all the ones before it put together. [3]
Visual: bars | ₹1 lakh at 12% (₹ lakh) | Yr 6: 2 | Yr 12: 3.9 | Yr 18: 7.7 | Yr 24: 15.2 | Yr 30: 30

## The fine print
Twelve percent is just an example, with no fees or taxes. Real investment values can fall; this smooth curve is an assumption. [3]
Visual: text | 12% is an example, *not a promise*

## What to do with it
Try the calculator with a lower rate, and compare twenty years with thirty. You'll see how much the extra time changes the result. [4]
Visual: text | Change the *rate*. Compare the *time*.
Source: Investor.gov compound interest calculator
